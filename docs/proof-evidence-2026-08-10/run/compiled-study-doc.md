## Pearls

- ★★★ **[4:04](#t=244) PEEP keeps alveoli open (recruited)** — Positive end-expiratory pressure keeps small airways and alveolar clusters open at the end of exhalation, a process called recruitment, rather than letting them collapse to zero pressure. Higher PEEP recruits more alveoli and therefore improves oxygenation.
- ★★★ **[7:10](#t=430) Rate+Vt affect CO₂; FiO₂+PEEP affect O₂** — The first two AC ventilator parameters—backup rate and tidal volume—determine minute ventilation and thus CO₂ removal. The last two—FiO₂ and PEEP—primarily determine oxygenation.
- ★★★ **[13:53](#t=833) AC mode: volume set, pressure variable** — In AC mode the clinician dials in tidal volume; the resulting pressure is variable and depends on lung compliance. Decreased compliance (e.g., pulmonary edema, pneumothorax) yields higher pressure for the same volume.
- ★★ **[0:25](#t=25) CPAP = non-invasive, PEEP = invasive** — CPAP refers to continuous positive airway pressure delivered non-invasively via a face mask, while PEEP refers to the same continuous-pressure concept applied through an invasive ventilator circuit with an endotracheal tube.
- ★★ **[1:46](#t=106) CPAP/PEEP delivers constant pressure** — In CPAP/PEEP, you dial in a pressure rather than a volume. That pressure stays the same whether the patient is breathing in or out, maintaining airway pressure throughout the respiratory cycle.
- ★★ **[6:46](#t=406) Pressure support is a key weaning mode** — Pressure support delivers a set pressure with each patient-triggered breath; higher pressure yields larger breaths. It is a popular mode for weaning patients off the ventilator.
- ★★ **[12:25](#t=745) Negative deflection marks patient-triggered breath** — A patient-triggered breath shows a negative pressure deflection on the waveform; a ventilator-triggered (backup rate) breath lacks this negative deflection but otherwise looks the same.
- ★ **[14:04](#t=844) Peak vs plateau pressure distinction ahead** — There are two types of pressures to be concerned about in mechanical ventilation: peak pressure and plateau pressure. This distinction will be covered in the next lecture.

## Concept breakdown

### CPAP vs PEEP terminology ([0:22](#t=22), [0:45](#t=45))

CPAP is the term used when continuous positive pressure is delivered non-invasively via a mask; PEEP is the term for the same concept delivered through an invasive ventilator with an endotracheal tube.

The speaker clarifies that the term 'CPAP' is typically used when the continuous-pressure mode is non-invasive—i.e., delivered through a face mask (like a CPAP or BiPAP mask) rather than an endotracheal tube. When the same continuous-pressure concept is used on an intubated patient connected to a ventilator, it is referred to as 'PEEP.' Both are essentially the same mode of ventilation.

**Your take:** CPAP is the non-invasive name (mask) and PEEP is the invasive name (through a ventilator/ET tube) for the same continuous-pressure idea.

### Continuous pressure in CPAP/PEEP ([0:53](#t=53), [1:52](#t=112), [2:22](#t=142))

CPAP/PEEP delivers a set continuous pressure that remains the same during inspiration and exhalation; you dial in a pressure rather than a volume or rate.

In CPAP/PEEP mode, the clinician dials in a continuous pressure rather than a specific volume or vent setting. The pressure does not change whether the patient is breathing in or breathing out—they always receive the same amount of pressure. PEEP stands for 'positive end-expiratory pressure,' meaning that even at the very end of exhalation, there is still pressure left in the airways (e.g., if set to 5, there is still a pressure of 5 cm H₂O in the airways at end-exhalation instead of zero).

**Your take:** You dial in a pressure, not a volume — it stays the same whether the patient is breathing in or breathing out.

### CPAP and PEEP setting range ([2:02](#t=122))

CPAP and PEEP can each be set anywhere from 5 to 20 cm of water pressure.

The speaker states that CPAP can be set anywhere from 5 cm of water pressure up to 20, and PEEP can similarly be set from 5 to 20 cm H₂O. These pressures are present on both inspiration and exhalation.

**Your take:** Both CPAP and PEEP get dialed in somewhere in the 5 to 20 cm H2O range.

### PEEP alveolar recruitment ([3:52](#t=232), [4:09](#t=249))

PEEP keeps small distal airways and alveolar clusters open and recruited at end-exhalation, preventing collapse to zero pressure.

In invasive ventilation, the airways narrow down to tiny passages ending in grape-like clusters (alveoli). PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure) is effective at keeping those alveoli open and 'recruited.' To recruit more alveoli, the clinician can increase the PEEP setting on the ventilator, typically via its own dedicated dial.

**Your take:** PEEP holds the little alveolar clusters open at the end of exhale instead of letting them collapse — turning up PEEP recruits more of them.

### CPAP for obstructive sleep apnea ([3:36](#t=216))

In non-invasive mode, CPAP helps obstructive sleep apnea by keeping airway pressure high enough to hold the tongue forward and keep the airway open.

The speaker explains that in non-invasive mode, CPAP is beneficial for patients with obstructive sleep apnea because the continuous pressure in the airway keeps the tongue forward and maintains an open airway, preventing obstruction during sleep.

**Your take:** The constant pressure holds the tongue forward so the airway doesn't obstruct — that's why CPAP helps with sleep apnea specifically in the non-invasive setting.

### Pressure support ventilation ([5:06](#t=306), [5:28](#t=328), [5:54](#t=354), [6:40](#t=400))

Pressure support (PS) delivers a set pressure with each patient-triggered breath—unlike pressure control, you cannot set a rate. Higher pressure yields a larger breath. PS is a popular weaning mode.

Pressure support is similar to PEEP/CPAP but is delivered only on patient-initiated breaths. It is also like AC (assist control) except that instead of delivering a specific volume, it delivers a specific pressure support for each breath. The patient initiates all breaths; unlike pressure control, you cannot set a rate. Higher pressure support results in bigger breaths. Pressure support is a very popular weaning mode of ventilation.

**Your take:** Pressure support only fires on a patient-triggered breath and delivers a set pressure instead of a set volume — no backup rate, and it's a common weaning mode.

### Four ventilation modes compared ([6:50](#t=410))

The four modes discussed are AC (volume-based, patient-triggered with backup rate), pressure control (pressure-based, settable rate), CPAP (continuous pressure regardless of breathing phase), and pressure support (patient-triggered, pressure per breath).

The speaker reviews four ventilator modes covered so far: AC, pressure control, CPAP, and pressure support. Each mode differs in whether it targets volume or pressure, whether the patient or ventilator initiates breaths, and whether a rate can be set.

### PEEP recruits alveoli for oxygenation ([7:01](#t=421), [7:04](#t=424), [7:38](#t=458), [7:40](#t=460), [7:43](#t=463))

PEEP is the pressure left in the circuit at the end of exhalation, present to recruit alveoli. Higher PEEP recruits more alveoli and improves oxygenation.

At the end of exhalation, PEEP leaves a set amount of pressure in the circuit to recruit alveoli. The higher the PEEP dial, the more alveoli get recruited -- and more recruited alveoli means better oxygenation. (edited to lead with the clinical takeaway)

**Your take:** Higher PEEP recruits more alveoli, and more recruited alveoli means better oxygenation.
